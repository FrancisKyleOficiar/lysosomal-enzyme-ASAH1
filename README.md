# QUESTIONS

**1. What does your enzyme normally break down inside lysosomes?**
ASAH1 encodes acid ceramidase, which breaks down ceramide into sphingosine and a free fatty acid through hydrolysis. These products can be reused in lipid metabolism, helping maintain the normal balance of sphingolipids within the cell.

**4. Why can glycosylation matter for lysosomal proteins? Does finding a motif prove the protein is glycosylated?**
Glycosylation can help lysosomal proteins fold correctly, maintain stability, and undergo proper processing and transport through the cell's secretory pathway. For ASAH1, glycosylation is particularly relevant because the mature enzyme consists of an alpha subunit that is not glycosylated and a glycosylated beta subunit. 
However, finding a potential N-glycosylation motif does not automatically prove that the site is glycosylated. Actual glycosylation depends on factors such as protein folding, accessibility of the site, and cellular processing. Experimental evidence is needed to confirm modification at a particular site. 

**5. What could happen in the cell if your enzyme did not work properly?**
If acid ceramidase does not function properly, ceramide can accumulate inside lysosomes because it is not broken down efficiently. This disrupts normal lipid metabolism and can affect cell signaling, inflammation, and cell survival. Defects in ASAH1 are associated with Farber disease, a lysosomal storage disorder that can cause painful and deformed joints, hoarseness, and tissue damage. Certain ASAH1 variants are also associated with spinal muscular atrophy with progressive myoclonic epilepsy.
